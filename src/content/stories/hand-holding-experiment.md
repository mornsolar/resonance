---
title: "The Hand-Holding Experiment: My First Day of Unemployment"
date: 2026-08-03
summary: "On my first day without a job, the most important thing I did was hold my daughter's hand, and then watch what happened to technology for the rest of the day."
reflection: "What's the first thing you reach for in the morning, and who might you reach for instead?"
---

Today was my first day of unemployment.

And strangely, the most important thing I did was not update my CV, search for jobs, or work out how long our savings might last.

The most important thing I did was hold my daughter's hand.

* * *

For some time, I have had a hypothesis.

Perhaps children do not always turn to technology because they are deeply attracted to technology itself.

Perhaps sometimes they turn to it because technology is available at the exact moment when human attention is not.

So I decided to run a small experiment on myself.

Before I touched any device in the morning, I would first make a connection with one of my children.

Not instruct them.

Not entertain them.

Just connect.

That morning, I woke up at around half past eight. Alethia was already downstairs, lying on the sofa reading Harry Potter.

Normally, I might have picked up my phone without even thinking.

Instead, I picked up my copy of Hyperfocus, sat beside her and began to read.

I held her hand while we read.

She rubbed my hand gently with her fingers.

Nothing dramatic happened.

But it felt complete.

* * *

Then she started playing with my watch.

For a moment, I thought: well, that did not take long. Technology has already entered the experiment.

But then I realised that perhaps I had framed the question wrongly.

The question was not whether technology appeared.

The question was whether it pulled us apart.

So instead of taking the watch away, we used the stopwatch to play a game. We tried to stop it at exactly one second.

We laughed.

The technology was still there, but it was serving the connection rather than replacing it.

That changed the whole experiment for me.

* * *

Later, Alethia sat at her desk and began painting.

I tried to give her my full attention.

I watched the water move through the colours. I warned her when the brush was too wet. I offered to change the water. I gave a few opinions about the colours, although she quite rightly made her own decisions.

At one point, she told me that she could draw a unicorn facing one direction but not the other.

My first instinct was to reach for my phone and use it as a mirror.

Then I stopped.

I did not want to bring the phone into the moment unnecessarily. Once a phone appears, it can easily stop being a tool and become a doorway into something else.

So I went upstairs and found a small physical mirror.

In the end, she decided she did not need it.

She simply continued drawing the unicorn in the direction she already knew.

That stayed with me.

Sometimes technology does not solve a problem.

Sometimes I introduce technology before the problem has even decided whether it needs solving.

Later, we chose a recipe together. She checked the ingredients in the kitchen, worked out what we needed to buy and took the lead when we went shopping.

At around one o'clock, she eventually opened her laptop and started playing Roblox.

And I was fine with it.

By that point, technology was not the centre of her day.

It was one activity inside a morning that had already contained reading, touch, play, painting, planning, shopping and responsibility.

We agreed that she would play three rounds, around thirty minutes in total, and I would remind her when lunch was ready.

There was no argument.

No confiscation.

No speech about screen time.

Just an agreement.

* * *

But underneath this experiment was another thought that kept interrupting me.

Today was my first day without a job.

I worry about what that means for our family.

We have hoped that Alethia might have the same educational opportunities as her elder sister. Without an income, that suddenly feels less certain.

And I keep asking myself what I am really trying to provide.

A better school?

A stronger social circle?

A better career?

A better future?

But then—

What does a better future actually mean?

I do not mainly want her to earn more money.

I want her to care about people.

I want her to care about society and nature.

I want her to know how to live among others, how to contribute and how to build relationships that matter.

I want her to have good memories.

And then I saw the contradiction.

I could spend years worrying about how to give her a good future, while overlooking the good present I can give her today.

I cannot guarantee which school she will attend next year.

I cannot yet guarantee what my next job will be.

But I can sit beside her.

I can hold her hand.

I can step into her world before asking her to step away from a screen.

I still do not know whether holding a parent's hand genuinely reduces a child's need for digital stimulation.

Perhaps the warmth, security and physical presence fulfil something that a metal device can only distract from temporarily.

Or perhaps I am reading too much into one good morning.

That is why this is an experiment, not a conclusion.

Tomorrow, I will try again.

Not by banning technology.

Not by making another rule for my children.

But by changing the first thing I do myself.

Before reaching for a device, I will reach for them.
