title: "The Auntie's Stall"
date: 2026-09-07
summary: "A packed lunch rush, a room full of heads bent over phones, and the twenty minutes my parents still talk about."
reflection: "The next time you're waiting somewhere with your head down, what else could those twenty minutes be for?"
---

> 咦?怎麼不是那個阿姨的檔口了?
>
> *"Huh — that's not the auntie's stall anymore?"*

Six months later, I walked back into the same place. The stall was gone.

The question rose in two minds at the same time — mine and my mother's. And my mother's memory is not good. She forgets most things.

She didn't forget this.

* * *

It was Chinese New Year, early 2026. I'd flown back from the UK. Rare time with my parents.

Lunch rush. Every table full. A queue two or three deep at the auntie's stall. Two stoves going at once. She was working both, alone, holding a small card — two columns, left and right — writing orders in a system only she could read. I still don't know how that system worked. Ours went in the right column.

We sat down to wait. And I looked around the room.

Everyone was on their phone. Heads down. Short videos, dramas, other people's lives. Waiting the way you're supposed to wait.

And a thought started and wouldn't leave: she has to cook all of this and carry every plate to every table, by herself. Is this what being a customer means? We paid, so we sit?

Then the honest part: I stopped listening to my parents. They were talking to me and I wasn't there. I sat in my own head for several minutes.

I told my mother I wanted to go and help.

> 別去啦。廚房裡東西那麼熱,萬一你被燙傷了怎麼辦。不要多管閒事 — 人家可能根本不想你幫忙。
>
> *"Don't. It's hot in there, what if you get burned? Don't meddle — she probably doesn't even want your help."*

* * *

我終於忍不住了. I finally couldn't sit still any longer.

I walked to the stall. "Auntie — I'm not saying you're slow. I'm just sitting there waiting anyway. What can I do? Can I help serve the others?"

And she laughed at me:

> 那你怎麼不早來?
>
> *"Well — why didn't you come earlier?"*

Not annoyed. Delighted. She pointed: the next order up was ours anyway. Go and get your own condiments. So I did. I plated up my own family's food, and I carried it.

Every single thing I was afraid of simply did not happen.

* * *

I turned around holding our food, and I walked back towards the table.

And my parents were looking at me.

It was not many steps. But it was enough distance to imagine myself as a parent — to imagine my own child deciding, unprompted, to go and help a stranger. And to know exactly how proud I would be.

That was when I knew I'd chosen right.

* * *

Instead of sitting there watching other people's exciting lives on a screen, I used the same twenty minutes to make a moment my parents will never forget.
